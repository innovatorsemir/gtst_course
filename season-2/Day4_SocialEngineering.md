# Day 4 – Social Engineering

## 1. What Is Social Engineering?

**Social engineering** is the act of manipulating people into performing actions or revealing confidential information.

It involves trickery or deception to gather information, gain access to computer systems, or obtain sensitive data. In many cases, the attacker never meets the victim face-to-face.

Social engineering uses **psychological manipulation** to trick individuals into making security mistakes or giving away sensitive information.

### Why Do Attackers Use Social Engineering?

Today, hacking into large and well-protected technology companies can be difficult. However, even when an organization invests millions of dollars in software and security infrastructure, human beings can remain a weak point.

The purpose of social engineering is usually to:

- Trick people into revealing passwords and other sensitive information.
- Steal personal, financial, or confidential business information.
- Convince victims to install spyware or other malicious software.
- Gain unauthorized access to systems and networks.
- Manipulate employees into performing actions that compromise organizational security.

**Key idea:** An organization may have strong technical security, but a single human mistake can expose it to an attack.

## 2. Social Engineering Attack Lifecycle

The Social Engineering Attack Lifecycle consists of four main phases through which an attacker manipulates a target to achieve a malicious objective.

### A. Research / Investigation Phase

The attacker collects information about the target to identify possible vulnerabilities, weak points, and opportunities to exploit human behavior.

Information may be collected from:
- Social media platforms.
- Company websites.
- Public records.
- Professional networking platforms such as LinkedIn.
- Publicly available employee information.

### B. Hook Phase

The attacker establishes contact with the target and attempts to build trust.

The attacker may use a false identity or pretend to be a trusted person, such as a colleague, company representative, or service provider.

### C. Play Phase

The attacker exploits the trust established during the hook phase to carry out the intended malicious activity.

Possible objectives include:
- Stealing confidential information.
- Obtaining passwords.
- Convincing the victim to install malware.
- Gaining unauthorized access to a system.
- Manipulating the victim into transferring money.

### D. Exit Phase

The attacker concludes the operation and attempts to avoid detection.

This may involve hiding evidence of the attack or ending communication with the victim.

### Social Engineering Lifecycle Diagram

**INVESTIGATION → HOOK → PLAY → EXIT**

## 3. Types of Social Engineering Attacks

Social engineering attacks can be classified into different types based on the methods used to manipulate victims.

### 3.1 Phishing

**Phishing** is a social engineering attack in which an attacker tricks people into providing sensitive information, such as passwords, credit card numbers, or personal details, by pretending to be a trustworthy person or organization.

Phishing can be performed through:
- Email.
- Social media.
- Messaging applications.
- Malicious websites.

#### How Phishing Works

1. The attacker sends a message that appears to come from a legitimate company or website.
2. The message may contain a link to a fake website.
3. The fake website is designed to look similar to the real one.
4. The victim is asked to enter personal or financial information.
5. The attacker may use the collected information for identity theft, financial fraud, or unauthorized account access.

#### Common Types of Phishing

**A. Normal Phishing**

A general phishing attack that attempts to deceive many potential victims rather than targeting one specific individual.

**B. Spear Phishing**

A targeted phishing attack designed for a particular person or organization.

The attacker researches the target and creates a personalized message based on the victim's interests, job, responsibilities, or circumstances.

| Phishing | Spear Phishing |
|---|---|
| Targets a broad range of potential victims. | Targets a specific person or organization. |
| Often uses general messages. | Uses personalized messages. |
| Requires less information about an individual target. | Usually involves researching the target. |

### 3.2 Vishing

**Vishing** is a combination of the words **Voice + Phishing**.

It is a phone-based scam designed to trick victims into sharing personal or confidential information.

The attacker calls the victim and may pretend to be:
- A bank employee.
- A technical support representative.
- A government official.
- A company representative.

The attacker uses conversation and deception to gather sensitive information.

### 3.3 Shoulder Surfing

**Shoulder surfing** is a physical technique used to obtain confidential information by directly observing someone's screen, keyboard, or keypad.

For example, an attacker may watch someone enter a password, ATM PIN, or phone unlock code.

Shoulder surfing can happen in:
- Public places.
- Offices.
- Banks and ATMs.
- Internet cafés.
- Public transportation.

**Prevention:** Cover the keypad when entering a PIN and avoid displaying sensitive information where others can see it.

### 3.4 Dumpster Diving

**Dumpster diving** is a technique used to obtain information from discarded documents, rubbish, or recycling bins.

Organizations may dispose of documents containing sensitive information without properly destroying them.

An attacker who obtains these documents may discover:
- Employee names and job positions.
- Internal company information.
- Phone numbers and email addresses.
- Financial documents.
- Customer information.
- Confidential business records.

This information may help an attacker plan further attacks.

#### Digital Dumpster Diving

Digital dumpster diving refers to obtaining sensitive information from discarded, forgotten, or poorly protected electronic resources.

Examples include:
- Recovering information from discarded electronic devices.
- Finding sensitive files that were not properly deleted.
- Accessing poorly protected storage devices.
- Discovering information on outdated servers or websites.

### 3.5 Pretexting

**Pretexting** is a social engineering attack in which an attacker creates a believable situation or story to convince a victim to reveal valuable information or perform an action.

The attacker may pretend to be someone the victim trusts or expects to communicate with.

#### Common Pretexting Attacks

**A. Romance Scam**

A romance scam is a social engineering attack that manipulates a victim's emotions, particularly feelings of love and trust.

The attacker creates a fake online identity and pretends to be romantically interested in the victim.

The scammer may spend weeks or months building trust before requesting money for:
- An emergency.
- A plane ticket.
- Medical expenses.
- Gifts or other financial needs.

**B. Grandparent Scam**

In a grandparent scam, an attacker gathers information about a target and their relatives before pretending to be a grandchild or another family member.

The attacker may create a fake profile using stolen information and photographs.

The attacker then contacts the victim and claims to be experiencing an emergency, such as:
- Trouble at school.
- A car accident.
- An urgent financial problem.

The victim is pressured to send money quickly and may be told to keep the situation secret.

**C. Cryptocurrency Scam**

Cryptocurrency scams use false identities and promises of financial rewards to manipulate people interested in cryptocurrency investments.

An attacker may pretend to be a wealthy or experienced investor and convince victims to invest money through a fraudulent opportunity.

After receiving the money, the scammer may disappear.

**D. Whaling Attack**

A **whaling attack** is a targeted phishing or social engineering attack directed at high-level individuals within an organization, such as CEOs, executives, or senior managers.

Attackers may:
- Pretend to be company leaders.
- Target senior executives directly.
- Request confidential business information.
- Attempt to obtain large financial payments through fake business transactions.

Whaling is often considered a specialized form of spear phishing because it targets high-profile individuals.

**E. Impersonation**

Impersonation occurs when an attacker pretends to be a trusted person, such as:
- A friend.
- A colleague.
- A company employee.
- A contractor.
- A technical support worker.

The attacker uses the false identity to gain trust and obtain information or access.

In an organizational environment, impersonation may be used to request confidential documents, gain access to systems, or convince employees to perform unsafe actions.

### 3.6 Baiting

**Baiting** is a social engineering attack that uses a false promise or attractive offer to exploit a victim's curiosity or greed.

The attacker offers something appealing to encourage the victim to take an unsafe action.

Examples include:
- Fake free software downloads.
- Free movies or games.
- Fake prizes and gifts.
- Promises of free products.
- Infected USB drives left in places where people may find them.

The victim may download malicious software, connect an infected device, or reveal personal information.

One common form of baiting uses physical storage media, such as USB drives, to spread malware.

### 3.7 Scareware

**Scareware** is a type of deception that frightens users with false security warnings or fictitious threats.

The victim is made to believe that their computer is infected or that their personal information is in danger.

The attacker then encourages the victim to install software or purchase a fake security product.

The software may provide no real protection and could itself contain malware.

Scareware is also known as:
- Deception software.
- Rogue scanner software.
- Fraudware.

**Example:** A website displays a fake warning saying, "Your computer is infected! Download this antivirus immediately."

### 3.8 Eavesdropping

**Eavesdropping** is secretly listening to private conversations to obtain sensitive information.

In social engineering, attackers may gather information by listening to physical or verbal communication.

This can happen:
- During conversations in public places.
- In offices and workplaces.
- Through unsecured communication channels.
- Through devices that capture conversations.

The information collected may include passwords, business plans, personal details, or confidential discussions.

## 4. Example: Social Engineering and Malware Attack Scenario

The following scenario demonstrates how social engineering can be combined with malware.

### Research Phase

The attacker gathers information about an employee from LinkedIn and other social media platforms.

The attacker discovers that the employee works in the finance department and regularly handles invoices.

### Hook Phase

The attacker sends a spear-phishing email to the employee while pretending to be a company vendor.

The email requests payment for a fake invoice.

### Play Phase

The employee opens the email and clicks a link to download the supposed invoice.

The downloaded file contains malware, which compromises the employee's computer and may provide unauthorized access to company systems.

### Exit Phase

The attacker attempts to hide evidence of the malware installation and avoid detection.

This scenario demonstrates how information gathering, impersonation, phishing, and malware can work together in a single attack.

## 5. Social Engineering in Security Testing

Social engineering can also be studied and tested as part of an authorized security assessment.

An organization may hire a cybersecurity professional to evaluate how employees respond to suspicious messages, phone calls, or other manipulation attempts.

The purpose of an authorized social engineering assessment is to:
- Identify weaknesses in employee awareness.
- Evaluate security procedures.
- Test how employees verify unusual requests.
- Improve organizational security awareness.
- Recommend better prevention measures.

These tests should be conducted with proper authorization and clearly defined boundaries.

## 6. Real-World Example: Uber Social Engineering Attack (2022)

The 2022 Uber cybersecurity incident is an example of how social engineering can be used to compromise an organization.

The incident involved an attacker who used social engineering to manipulate an employee and gain access to internal systems.

It demonstrates that attackers may target employees rather than directly attacking an organization's technical infrastructure.

**Main lesson:** Strong technical security must be supported by employee awareness, identity verification, and effective access controls.

## 7. Prevention of Social Engineering Attacks

Social engineering attacks can be reduced through employee awareness, strong authentication, careful communication, and good security practices.

### 7.1 Employee Awareness

Employees and individuals should understand common social engineering techniques.

They should be trained to recognize:
- Suspicious emails.
- Fake websites.
- Unexpected phone calls.
- Unusual requests for money.
- Requests for passwords or confidential information.
- Messages that create urgency or fear.

### 7.2 Multi-Step Verification

Use multi-factor authentication (MFA) to add an extra layer of account protection.

Even if an attacker obtains a password, an additional authentication requirement can make unauthorized access more difficult.

### 7.3 Strong Password Policy

Use strong and unique passwords.

A good password should:
- Be difficult to guess.
- Avoid personal information such as names and birthdays.
- Avoid common words and predictable patterns.
- Include a suitable combination of letters, numbers, and symbols.
- Not be reused across multiple accounts.

### 7.4 Change Default Passwords

Always replace default passwords on devices, applications, and accounts.

Default credentials are often publicly known and may be easy for attackers to discover.

### 7.5 Use Password Managers

A password manager securely stores passwords and helps users create and maintain unique passwords for different accounts.

Password managers are useful across:
- Desktop computers.
- Tablets.
- Mobile phones.

A password vault is a secure storage system for passwords and other authentication information.

### 7.6 Check Links Before Clicking

Avoid clicking suspicious links in emails, messages, or social media posts.

Before opening a link:
- Check the sender's identity.
- Examine the website address.
- Look for misspellings or unusual domains.
- Be careful with shortened links.
- Confirm unexpected requests through an independent communication channel.

A link inspection service mentioned in the lecture:

https://redirectdetective.com

**Important:** Link-checking services are not a guarantee of safety. A link that appears safe may still lead to a malicious or compromised website.

### 7.7 Do Not Trust Unexpected Requests Automatically

Do not assume that a message is genuine simply because it comes from a familiar name or account.

An attacker's message may come from:
- A compromised friend's account.
- A fake employee profile.
- An impersonated manager.
- A fraudulent company account.

Verify unusual requests independently, especially requests involving passwords, money, or confidential information.

### 7.8 Be Careful With Password-Checking Websites

The lecture mentions the following website:

https://www.passwordmonster.com

Password-checking websites may provide an estimate of password strength. However, users should avoid entering their actual passwords into unfamiliar third-party websites.

A safer practice is to use a trusted password manager's built-in password-strength checker or a reputable offline password-strength tool.

## 9. Quick Summary

- **Social Engineering:** Manipulating people into revealing confidential information or performing unsafe actions.
- **Purpose:** Steal information, obtain passwords, install malware, or gain unauthorized access.
- **Attack Lifecycle:** Investigation → Hook → Play → Exit.
- **Phishing:** Deceiving victims through fake messages or websites.
- **Spear Phishing:** Targeting a specific individual or organization.
- **Vishing:** Voice-based phishing through phone calls.
- **Shoulder Surfing:** Secretly observing someone entering confidential information.
- **Dumpster Diving:** Collecting sensitive information from discarded documents or digital resources.
- **Pretexting:** Creating a believable false situation to gain information or trust.
- **Whaling:** Targeting senior executives and other high-level individuals.
- **Impersonation:** Pretending to be a trusted person.
- **Baiting:** Using attractive offers to trick victims into unsafe actions.
- **Scareware:** Using fake security warnings to frighten users.
- **Eavesdropping:** Secretly listening to conversations to collect information.
- **Prevention:** Employee awareness, MFA, strong passwords, password managers, link verification, and independent identity checks.

**Key Lesson:** Human awareness is an essential part of cybersecurity. Strong technical defenses alone cannot prevent every attack if people are easily manipulated.
